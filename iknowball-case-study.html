Overview

Why this project exists

Sneakers and basketball go hand in hand, and I've always enjoyed draft-and-guess basketball games, the kind where you pull a random real team and see how far you can take it. 82-0.com is the one I kept coming back to, until the same handful of teams and eras kept showing up over and over, undermining the entire premise of a "random draft" game. So I built and shipped iknowball: draft a 5-man NBA roster one position at a time from randomly rolled real teams across six decades, then get scored on how that roster would perform across an 82-game season.

Research

Who I Talked To

Research happened in two rounds. First, four unmoderated interviews (Maddy, Arnold, Justin, and Nicky) spanning basketball knowledge levels from none to expert, testing version 1.0: a simple randomization ticker, a 10-player pool per spin, and an end-of-round breakdown of the score, best pick, and a better alternative. Then, after the first wave of fixes shipped, a 20-person structured playtest run by Kevin and his rec basketball league, the first round with real quantitative data and the first head-to-head test against 82-0 itself.

Asked why, unprompted, of the 7 who preferred iknowball over 82-0, they converged on the same four reasons:

  • The explanation of why the score came out the way it did, not just the final number
  • Cards visibly divided by grade/tier instead of one flat list
  • Novelty in the teams themselves: no repeats, where 82-0 was felt to recycle the same rosters
  • A mix that still felt current: enough modern teams that people actually recognized, alongside old-school ones

Findings

What actually came out of it

Raw notes, grouped into the themes that repeated across all three rounds. Quotes are lightly cleaned up from shorthand field notes, not paraphrased.

MaddyArnold

Confusion with game mechanic

  • The single biggest usability failure in the first round: casual and non-knowledgeable players couldn't tell a randomized event was happening at all.
  • Arnold: couldn't tell he could interact with anything, only realizing he could spin after clicking around, and didn't understand it was a randomization until his second spin.
  • Maddy: found clicking "Draft This Team" every round repetitive with no sense of anticipation; suggested the screen "sit on team and decade for 3 seconds."
  • Arnold, later: explicitly asked for "more clarity that it is a roll," a literal spin animation, and 3D dice instead of a flat 2D reveal.
MaddyJustin

Stats were present but invisible

  • Maddy: "stats are hard to see." She was already relying on them since she couldn't judge players by reputation, and they still weren't legible.
  • Justin, someone who actively evaluates players by stats and role, didn't notice stats were on the cards at all in his first pass. "Make that more obvious."
Arnold

Visual identity felt generic

  • Wanted a basketball court / hardwood background instead of a blank screen.
  • Wanted player positions shown spatially, not just as a row of pills.
  • Asked for "moments of delight," transitions, and more visual stimulation generally.
JustinArnoldNickyKevin's group

Scoring needs to be transparent, not just present

  • Justin liked the post-game breakdown enough to explicitly connect his own score to the feedback he received, proof the explanation was landing.
  • Arnold independently suggested "build a system where it grades off high scores" before learning one already existed.
  • Nicky, in the follow-up round, pushed further, asking: "feedback is just looking at stats though." Do we actually reward players who played together, or only aggregate stat totals the way 82-0 does? She also asked directly whether scoring is "too strict."
  • Kevin's group confirmed this is the single biggest reason people preferred iknowball over 82-0 at all: 20/20 said they enjoyed the end-of-round explanation of why the score came out the way it did. It's the clearest, most quantified validation this feature has gotten.
JustinNicky

The player pool feels thin

  • Justin: wants more players, wants pictures of each player, wants "more imagery and not as much text."
  • Nicky said, independently, "likes bigger pool of players," the same gap Justin had already flagged, unprompted, in a later round.
ArnoldKevin's group

Wants for delight and reward

  • A 2K-style animated transition between the last pick and the results screen.
  • Confetti / celebration for a good record, a "womp womp" for a bad one.
  • Easter eggs, specifically named NBA meme references (Inside the NBA clips, LeBron/Tatum reaction clips).
  • "Add dopamine boosters to feel more rewarding for feedback."
  • Kevin's group said the same thing independently, at a much larger sample: "gamify it more," plus three concrete, new mechanic requests rather than just polish.
  • Flagged as an IP/right-of-publicity risk: real player photos and named meme clips aren't something I can implement literally as described. Still an open decision.
Arnold

Copy clarity

  • "Draft from this team" tested clearer than "Draft This Team" as the button label.
  • Wanted more explicit instructions surfaced near the top of the screen.
Founding complaintNickyKevin's group

Repetition: the reason the project exists, twice over

  • The entire premise of iknowball vs. 82-0 was "stop giving me the same teams over and over."
  • Nicky, independently, in the round after the first rotation fix shipped: still getting repeats, specifically calling out decades as well as teams. "Likes old school teams, keep it randomized so no repeats," while also suggesting less weight on eras "we care less about."
  • Kevin's group reconfirmed it again, at n=20, after both the era-rotation fix and further tuning had already shipped: the same team could still land on the same decade again almost immediately. "More randomization of teams so that there are no repetitive teams" and, separately, "makes it more fun when I can try different combos." Variety isn't a nice-to-have here. It's the actual product thesis.
Kevin's group

Ratings still don't feel accurate: the "decade dilution" pattern

  • The clearest, most repeated complaint in the n=20 round: real stars were rated far below what their actual careers earned, because their stat line was averaged across an entire decade instead of their real peak season.
  • Specific misses flagged this round: 2020s Zion Williamson, 2010s Kyrie Irving, 2000s Jermaine O'Neal, and a repeat flag on 2020s Jalen Brunson (already fixed by the time this round's notes came in).
  • The group's own words, twice, independently: "the problem is the decade dilution." Now the standing name for this bug class.
Kevin's group

The game needs more meta-game, not just more polish

  • Distinct from the delight wishlist above: structural, competitive-loop requests from people who already play games like this seriously.
  • A leaderboard.
  • Show the actual odds of rolling a given player/team, rather than leaving the mechanic invisible.
  • Make legendary championship-caliber teams roll at lower odds than an average team from the same era. Rarity should track real-world greatness, not just decade.
  • A 1v1 head-to-head mode against another drafted roster, not just a solo 82-game simulation.
ArnoldKevin's group

Difficulty is still calibrated around basketball literacy

  • Arnold's original struggle with the interface, echoed at n=20: Kevin found the game noticeably harder with older teams he doesn't recognize.
  • The group also wants the game to stay hard ("balance between making it hard but not too easy"). Not a request to make old eras easier, but to make difficulty intentional rather than an accident of which decade got rolled.

Process

Research → decision → ship

Foundation already in place going into this research: four scoring pillars (Scoring, Rebounding, Defense, Team Chemistry), a rule-based post-game breakdown, tier cards shown only after the draft, and a full 168-roster data audit. Everything below is what changed after real players got their hands on it.

Shipped

The roll: from a text ticker to a tumbling 3D die

  • Finding: players couldn't tell a randomized roll was happening at all, especially casual and non-knowledgeable testers.
  • Decision: a basketball-textured 3D die that tumbles through team/era combos before locking on the result, synced to the old ticker's decelerating cadence.
  • A later polish pass fixed three animation bugs it surfaced along the way, tightening wobble amplitude from 24° to 9°.
Explored, reverted

Half-court background and position tracker

  • Explored to answer requests for a court-like background and a spatial (not row-based) position display.
  • Reverted the same day: the added height forced scrolling on every single round, breaking game pacing.
  • Salvaged as a one-time decorative background on the Start Game screen, which only appears once per session.
Shipped

Stat legibility: from inline text to standout-highlighted stat blocks

  • Finding: stats were hard to see, and one stat-driven tester didn't notice they existed on the card at all.
  • Decision: a bold number and label per stat, plus a computed "standout" badge (Rebounder, Playmaker, Rim Protector...) per player, reusing the same real-data ceilings already powering the scoring model.
Shipped

The founding bug: decades weren't rotating across games

  • Finding: the exact complaint that started the project, resurfacing. Franchises rotated across games; decades never did.
  • Decision: gave decades the same cross-game rotation logic franchises already had.
  • Verified with an 8-game simulation: 30/30 franchises cycled before repeat, 6/6 eras surfaced every cycle, including the 1970s and 1980s.
Shipped

The rating-accuracy audit: naming and fixing "decade dilution"

  • Finding: real stars were rated far below their actual careers, because one universal stat-weight formula punished pure scorers for categories outside their role, and a player's listed stat line was averaged across an entire decade instead of their peak season.
  • Decision: position-specific scoring weights, multi-position players scored against their best-fit position, and ~37 player entries corrected across two audit sweeps.
  • Still open: Kevin's group's newest flags (Zion Williamson, Kyrie Irving, Jermaine O'Neal) are queued for the next pass.
Deliberate override

Jalen Brunson: when the formula and reality disagree

  • Finding: Brunson's real career-best season (2023-24) scored 49.8, 2.2 points short of Diamond, despite leading the Knicks to a championship and winning Finals MVP.
  • Decision: one manually-forced exception to Diamond (96), the only override in the entire rating system. Every other "formula vs. trophy" disagreement was left formula-pure.
Shipped

Tier-rating restructure: giving each rarity its own lane

  • Finding: Silver, Gold, and Diamond shared one continuous 40-99 curve, so a fresh Diamond could score as low as 85, barely distinct from a strong Gold.
  • Decision: fixed, non-overlapping bands per tier (Silver 40-79, Gold 80-89, Diamond 90-99), verified across the full ~1,690-player dataset.
Shipped

Team/decade repetition, round two: a per-franchise memory

  • Finding: Kevin's group reconfirmed the founding complaint even after the first rotation fix. A team could still land on the same decade again almost immediately.
  • Decision: added a lightweight per-franchise memory that heavily penalizes (not bans) a team's most recent era on its next roll.
  • Verified with a 300-game simulation: same team/decade back-to-back dropped from 11 instances to 1, with zero regression to franchise rotation.

Takeaway

Noticing the flaw is easy. Shipping the fix is the actual work.

Anyone who plays 82-0 could tell you it repeats itself too much. The harder part was building an alternative good enough that real players preferred it, then discovering my own fix had quietly reintroduced a narrower version of the exact bug I built the whole game to solve, and fixing that too.

A game you actually play is the best source of product instinct you'll ever have. I didn't need a research brief to know 82-0 was repetitive. I needed the discipline to build the fix, ship it, and keep listening once real people started playing it.
