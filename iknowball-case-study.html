Fixing a broken game

I kept getting the same handful of teams and eras every time I played 82-0.com, so I built and shipped my own version instead. This is what happened after real players got their hands on it: four in-depth interviews, a 20-person playtest with basketball enthusiasts, and a running data-accuracy audit that turned into 15 real (and counting), shipped rounds of improvement.

Overview

Why this project exists

Sneakers and basketball go hand in hand, and I've always enjoyed draft-and-guess basketball games, the kind where you pull a random real team and see how far you can take it. 82-0.com is the one I kept coming back to, until I noticed the same handful of teams and eras kept showing up over and over, undermining the entire premise of a "random draft" game. So I built my own version and shipped it: iknowball (a play on the modern term), a web game where players draft a 5-man NBA roster one position at a time, pulling from randomly rolled real teams across six decades, then get scored on how that roster would actually perform across an 82-game season.

That founding complaint has now come back around twice. The first time, it turned out iknowball had quietly reintroduced the exact repetition problem it was built to fix: teams rotated correctly across games, but decades didn't. The second time, it came from an entirely new, much larger source: a 20-person playtest run by a hoop group who actually know the fantasy-draft genre. They found a narrower version of the same bug, the same team landing on the same decade again almost immediately, even after the first fix had already shipped.

This update folds that 20-person round into the original research, and adds everything shipped since: a full rating-accuracy audit (fixing the "decade dilution" pattern this document's earlier draft hadn't yet named), a deliberate one-off rating override, a full restructure of the tier-rating scale, and the second, deeper fix to team/decade repetition. Everything below is organized the way the work actually happened: research first, then cross-cutting findings, then a section-by-section account of what shipped, reverted, or is still on the roadmap, with the real numbers behind each call.

Research

Who I Talked To

Two rounds: four unmoderated deep-dive sessions spanning basketball knowledge levels, then a 20-person structured playtest with people who actually play fantasy-style basketball games for fun.

User research and feedback was tested on version 1.0 of iknowball. Version 1.0 consists of a simple ticker for randomization of team/decade, a pool of 10 players for each spin, and an explanation at the end for the score, the best player picked, and an alternative pick that would have been better.

Asked why, unprompted, of the 7 who preferred iknowball over 82-0, they converged on the same four reasons:

  • The explanation of why the score came out the way it did, not just the final number
  • Cards visibly divided by grade/tier instead of one flat list
  • Novelty in the teams themselves: no repeats, where 82-0 was felt to recycle the same rosters
  • A mix that still felt current: enough modern teams that people actually recognized, alongside old-school ones

Findings

What actually came out of it

Raw notes, grouped into the themes that repeated across all three rounds. Quotes are lightly cleaned up from shorthand field notes, not paraphrased.

MaddyArnold

The roll didn't read as a roll

The single biggest usability failure in the first round: casual and non-knowledgeable players couldn't tell a randomized event was happening at all.

  • Arnold: couldn't tell he could interact with anything, only realizing he could spin after clicking around, and didn't understand it was a randomization until his second spin.
  • Maddy: found clicking "Draft This Team" every round repetitive with no sense of anticipation; suggested the screen "sit on team and decade for 3 seconds."
  • Arnold, later: explicitly asked for "more clarity that it is a roll," a literal spin animation, and 3D dice instead of a flat 2D reveal.
MaddyJustin

Stats were present but invisible

  • Maddy: "stats are hard to see." She was already relying on them since she couldn't judge players by reputation, and they still weren't legible.
  • Justin, someone who actively evaluates players by stats and role, didn't notice stats were on the cards at all in his first pass. "Make that more obvious."
Arnold

Visual identity felt generic

  • Wanted a basketball court / hardwood background instead of a blank screen.
  • Wanted player positions shown spatially, not just as a row of pills.
  • Asked for "moments of delight," transitions, and more visual stimulation generally.

Scoring needs to be transparent, not just present

  • Justin liked the post-game breakdown enough to explicitly connect his own score to the feedback he received, proof the explanation was landing.

Repetition: the reason the project exists, twice over

  • The entire premise of iknowball vs. 82-0 was "stop giving me the same teams over and over."
  • Nicky, independently, in the round after the first rotation fix shipped: still getting repeats, specifically calling out decades as well as teams. "Likes old school teams, keep it randomized so no repeats," while also suggesting less weight on eras "we care less about."
  • Kevin's group reconfirmed it again, at n=20, after both the era-rotation fix and further tuning had already shipped: the same team could still land on the same decade again almost immediately. "More randomization of teams so that there are no repetitive teams" and, separately, "makes it more fun when I can try different combos." Variety isn't a nice-to-have here. It's the actual product thesis.
Kevin's group

Ratings still don't feel accurate: the "decade dilution" pattern

The clearest, most repeated complaint in the n=20 round, and the one the Process section below leans on most: real stars were showing up rated far below what their actual careers earned, because their listed stat line was quietly averaged across an entire decade instead of reflecting their real peak season.

  • Specific misses flagged this round: 2020s Zion Williamson, 2010s Kyrie Irving, 2000s Jermaine O'Neal, and a repeat flag on 2020s Jalen Brunson (already fixed by the time this round's notes came in, see the Process section below).
  • The group's own words, twice, independently: "the problem is the decade dilution." That's now the standing name for this bug class in this project.
Kevin's group

The game needs more meta-game, not just more polish

Distinct from the "delight" wishlist above, these are structural, competitive-loop requests from people who already play games like this seriously.

  • A leaderboard.
  • Show the actual odds of rolling a given player/team, rather than leaving the mechanic invisible.
  • Make legendary championship-caliber teams roll at lower odds than an average team from the same era. Rarity should track real-world greatness, not just decade.
  • A 1v1 head-to-head mode against another drafted roster, not just a solo 82-game simulation.
ArnoldKevin's group

Difficulty is still calibrated around basketball literacy

Arnold's original struggle with the interface, echoed at n=20: Kevin specifically found the game noticeably harder with older teams, since he doesn't recognize as many of those rosters. Balanced against the group also wanting the game to stay hard ("balance between making it hard but not too easy"), this isn't a request to make old eras easier. It's a request to make sure the difficulty is intentional rather than an accident of which decade got rolled.

Process

Research → decision → ship

Each item below moved from a mockup (or several) to a real, verified change: shipped, rejected by real use and reverted, or in one case, deliberately overridden against the formula's own output. Numbers are pulled directly from build verification and simulation, not estimated after the fact.

Shipped

The roll: from a text ticker to a tumbling 3D die

Directly answers Arnold's "more clarity that it is a roll" and "make it dice more 3D." Explored as static mockups before touching production code: a plain casino die, a "team cube" where the six faces were the six candidate teams, and finally the shipped direction: a basketball-textured die that tumbles, flashes a new team/era combo on every face each tick (same decelerating cadence the old text ticker used), then locks on the real result while the existing reveal card drops in beneath it.

Explored
4 static background treatments → literal court diagram → basketball-textured die vs. "team-face" die → combined die + text-ticker motion
Shipped
3D CSS cube (no libraries), Web Animations API-driven tumble, synced to the same 55ms→230ms decelerating tick the original ticker used
Caught in build
Full mockup size (160×120px cube) overflowed the viewport by 242px on first real implementation, the exact same scroll regression a separate court-visual experiment had already been reverted for.
first pass: 962px content vs 720px viewport compressed to cube 76px → exact fit user asked to see full size → measured 26px desktop / 3px mobile overflow shipped at full 120px cube once overflow was confirmed negligible
Explored, reverted

Half-court background and position tracker

Answers Arnold's "make the background like a basketball court" and the request to show positions spatially. Built a half-court diagram with all five draft positions placed on it, replacing the horizontal position-tracker row, filling in with the drafted player's name as picks locked. Shipped, then reverted the same day.

Why reverted
The court diagram added enough vertical height that the page required scrolling on every single round. "It throws off the balance of the game because you have to scroll now." Reverted fully to the original pill-row tracker.
Salvaged
The same court asset was repurposed as a one-time decorative background on the Start Game screen only, since that screen appears once instead of once per round, so there's no scroll cost.
Shipped

Stat legibility: from inline text to standout-highlighted stat blocks

Answers both Maddy ("stats are hard to see") and Justin (didn't notice stats existed). Seven presentation concepts were mocked before building: the current baseline, big-number stat blocks, magnitude bars, icon glyphs, color-intensity "heat pills," a headline-stat-only progressive-disclosure card, and a stat-block-plus-standout-badge combo. The last was chosen.

Shipped
Each stat gets its own bold number + tiny label; each player's single standout category is computed and called out with a badge ("Rebounder," "Playmaker," "Rim Protector"...) and a highlighted number.
Data source
The "standout" computation reuses CATEGORY_CEILINGS, the same position-adjusted, real-data-driven ceilings already powering the scoring model, rather than inventing new arbitrary thresholds.
Bug caught in build
The standout badge overlapped long player names under absolute positioning. Fixed by making name + badge a proper flex row instead.
Shipped

The founding bug, round one: decades weren't rotating across games

Directly answers the complaint that motivated the whole project, resurfacing independently through Nicky: "likes old school teams, keep it randomized so no repeats." Diagnosis: franchises already had a cross-game rotation (stored locally, cycling through all 30 before any repeat), but eras had no cross-game memory at all, only a static weighting toward the 2000s/2010s/2020s. Teams varied; decades didn't.

Fix
Built the same rotation mechanism for eras that franchises already had: exclude each decade from future rolls once seen, until all six have appeared, then reset.
Verification
Scripted an 8-game simulation rather than relying on manual play-testing.
30/30 franchises cycled before any repeat 6/6 eras surfaced every cycle, incl. 1970s/1980s 1970s prior weight: 0.02 vs. 2000s-2020s at 1.6 each
Shipped

Animation polish and three bugs it surfaced

A follow-up request to make the die tumble "more 3D" led to a genuinely bug-prone build cycle worth documenting for what it caught, not just what shipped.

Bug 1
React's StrictMode was double-invoking the roll effect in dev, silently running two competing animations on the same die at once. Fixed by cancelling the previous animation on cleanup.
Bug 2
An added wobble axis occasionally rotated the cube through an orientation where it visually collapsed into a flat "open book" shape. Root cause: interpolating three separate axis rotations between too few, too-large keyframe jumps.
Bug 3
A more "correct" quaternion-based fix was attempted and introduced a worse bug (the die vanishing entirely at certain frames), so it was rolled back in favor of a simpler, denser-keyframe approach instead.
keyframes: 6 → 36 (abandoned) → 30 (shipped) wobble amplitude: 24° → 9° verified by freezing the animation at ~15 timestamps across the full 2s roll

The rating-accuracy audit: naming and fixing "decade dilution"

The direct response to "should adjust ratings of some players as it doesn't seem accurate," first raised generically, then repeatedly with specific names attached: 90s Reggie Miller, 2010 Joakim Noah, 2020 Paolo Banchero and Cade Cunningham, 2010 LeBron, 2020 Jalen Brunson, 2000 Yao Ming, and eventually Kevin's group's own list. Two root causes were found and fixed, plus a full sweep of individual player entries.

Root cause 1
Scoring used one universal stat-weight set for every position, so pure scorers (Reggie Miller) were punished for weak rebounding/blocks that were never relevant to their role. Replaced with position-specific weights.
Root cause 2
A multi-position player (e.g. LeBron, listed PG/SG/SF/PF/C for draft eligibility) was being scored only against his first listed position, the worst possible weight set for his actual game. Fixed to score against whichever listed position rates him highest. LeBron's 2010s Cavaliers entry alone went from 86 to 99 off this fix.
Data pattern
"Decade dilution": a player's listed stat line was an average across an entire decade of play, including decline years, instead of their real peak or best-representative season. This silently capped stars whose careers had a clear rise-and-fall shape.
Fixed this pass
~37 individual player entries corrected across two audit sweeps, each verified against real per-season stats: Yao Ming, Tony Parker, Blake Griffin, LaMarcus Aldridge, Manu Ginobili, Nikola Vucevic, Danny Granger, Larry Nance, Vin Baker, Kevin Willis, Tom Chambers, Doug Collins, Buck Williams, Kevin Love (both his Cleveland and Minnesota entries), Scottie Barnes, Evan Mobley, Shai Gilgeous-Alexander's MVP season, and more.
Still open
Kevin's group's newest examples (2020 Zion Williamson, 2010 Kyrie Irving, 2000 Jermaine O'Neal) are queued for the next pass, not yet fixed as of this writing.
example: Yao Ming 2000s, 19.5 → 25.0 pts (real 2006-07 peak): Silver → Gold example: Scottie Barnes 2020s, 16.5 → 19.9 pts (real 2023-24 season): Silver → Gold ~37 player entries corrected
Deliberate override

Jalen Brunson: when the formula and reality disagree

Jalen Brunson led the Knicks to their first championship in 50 years and won 2026 Finals MVP. Those are real events, not a hypothetical. His actual career-best regular season (28.7 pts / 3.6 reb / 6.7 ast / 0.9 stl / 0.2 blk, 2023-24) still scores 49.8 under the position-weighted formula, 2.2 points short of the Diamond cutoff, because the point-guard weights lean hard on steals, a stat his game never emphasized.

Precedent
Every other "formula disagrees with the trophy" case this project has hit (Derrick Rose, Jalen Brunson's own earlier gold rating, Joakim Noah) was left formula-pure, on the stated principle that letting stats override a trophy keeps the game's integrity intact.
This time
Given the scale of the real accomplishment, I made the call to manually force this one entry to Diamond (96), a named, commented, single-player exception in the code, not a change to the formula itself.
formula score: 49.8 (Gold) manual override: 96 (Diamond) 1 of 1 manual overrides in the entire rating system
Shipped

Tier-rating restructure: giving each rarity its own lane

A direct request, separate from any single research quote: the rating scale had Silver, Gold, and Diamond all sharing one continuous 40-99 curve, which meant a player who'd only just crossed into Diamond could show as low as 85, barely distinguishable from a strong Gold and nowhere near what a "Diamond" rarity should feel like.

Fix
Each tier now gets its own fixed, non-overlapping numeric band, proportionally scaled within its own score range rather than the shared curve.
Silver: 40-79 Gold: 80-89 Diamond: 90-99
Before One shared 40-99 curve. Lowest real Diamond: 85. Highest real Gold: 84. Ratings 85-89 were dead space nothing could ever land on.
After Three fixed bands, verified across the full ~1,690-player dataset. Every Diamond is 90+, every Gold tops out at 89, no gaps, no overlap.
Shipped

Team/decade repetition, round two: a per-franchise memory

Kevin's group reconfirmed the founding complaint after the first era-rotation fix had already shipped: the same team could still land on the same decade again almost immediately. The original fix tracked era freshness in aggregate; it had no memory that a specific franchise had just appeared as, say, its 1990s roster.

Fix
Added a lightweight per-franchise memory: whichever era a given team last appeared as gets heavily penalized (not banned) the next time that team is rolled. A single remembered fact per team, not a full rotation, so it can't reproduce the earlier "forced rare era" bug.
Verification
300-game simulation, before vs. after, on the exact production code.
same team + same decade, back-to-back games: 11 → 1 instance same combo within 2 games: 33 → 7 instances franchise hard-cycle: still 0 violations, all 30 teams perfectly even 1970s/80s rarity: unchanged, no regression
